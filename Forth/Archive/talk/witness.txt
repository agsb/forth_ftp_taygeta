I understand the phenomenon Yunki is talking about.  People do seem to become
addicted to the stuff that is FORTH.  On the Amiga, though there is a tendency
to use what has been done before due to the approach of shared libraries.  I
have MultiFORTH for the Amiga and it support libraries very well.  Sometime
soon I hope to get around to writing interface code for the arp.library
(AmigaDOS Replacement Project Library) which will give me access to printf(),
etc. as if I were using C.  Perhaps people who learn FORTH right after BASIC
don't see any need for other languages, but they do have their place.  Just
as FORTH has its place.  The problem with developing in other languages (at
least so far on the Amiga) is that you need beaucoup resources in order to
be productive.  (There is a thread in comp.sys.amiga addressing this through
incremental compilers.)

One appealing thing about FORTH is its elegance, simplicity and compactness
for implementation on most microprocessors.  My opinion is that FORTH is
preferable for small micros, especially (8-bit) embedded ones.  Here you
can have a small ROM/PROM/EEPROM containing the kernel, and you can
prototype your software in RAM, with the ROM providing for downloading over
a serial line.  It is possible, with the correct hardware, to have a fully
functioning micro running in just 3 chips using this method with FORTH.  Of
course the micro doesn't do anything really interesting because its not
connected to the real world except through your serial line.  Its much
easier to port FORTH to a new micro compared to C or any other ``high-level''
language.

As far as the Amiga goes, I'd be glad to start some kind of mailing list for
FORTH hackers on the Amiga if there is sufficient interest.  Again, I chose
FORTH (after experimenting with C) because I had a limited configuration Amiga
(512K, two floppies) that made developing in C excruciatingly slow.  With
FORTH, things are nice and quick.  Unfortunately now I don't have the time to
do any significant development :-(.  Well, I didn't mean to be this long, but
you get my point.

                                                        Rich Thomson

Dear Forth-ers,

Allow me to introduce myself: I'm an Amiga owner who has a C compiler and
an Amigized, subroutine-threaded forth called JForth (nice forth, by the
way). My native tongue is C. I find myself drawn to the simplicity,
extensibility, immediacy and structural elegance of the forth environment...
BUT...I find I can't express myself in it nearly as easily as I can in C.
Several reasons:

1) zillions of "words" to memorize, as opposed to a small, powerful 
   set of keywords.

2) lack (in most implementations) of local variables. (JForth does 
   support these, actually, but I haven't played with them yet)

3) lack of syntax, readability. I've been considering writing a lisp-like
   parser for it:  ' a b + c * d - ' becomes (- (* c (+ a b)) d)
   At least one can see precisely the relationship of operands to operators.

4) all the stack gymnastics involved. It seems like I spend more time
   trying to remember what's where on the stack than on any other aspect
   of forth program development.

5) isolationism; it's too integrated. I'd like to be able to produce
   binary objects and libraries that can be called from other languages.
   How about stand-alone applications that don't have the entire dictionary
   tagging along? Forth's threaded nature makes these things difficult,
   though not impossible. On the other side of the coin, it'd be nice to
   be able to load, link and call external binaries from forth.

I'm not bashing forth, not a'tall. I realize that facility with any language
is an acquired skill. What I'm trying to do is resolve this love-hate
relationship I have with forth. I would like to get to know it better, but
I'm repulsed by some of its awkward (in my view) aspects. I just don't feel
that I can write anything "serious" in forth. So, my question
to forth devotees (especially those who also speak C) is, how does one
develop facility with this odd, fascinating language? Practice, I know,
but how does one get past the weirdness and into the land of Productivity?
When will I get this religious experience I keep hearing about? Thanks.

        - Pete

I use Mult-Forth for the Amiga, myself.  It has local variables, which
I believe are similiar to the implementation in JForth.  At first I
avoided their use, but have changed my mind.

FORTH is most readable when each word is very small and does only one
specific thing.  Therefore, most words will not need local variables
because there won't be too much on the stack to keep track of with
ordinary stack operations.  But, for interfacing with the Amiga OS,
where lots of structures and the like have to be interfaced, and the OS
routines take lots of arguments, using local variables can make Forth
easier to use and much more readable.  

>

I personally would find this syntax distastful.  I believe part of the
trick to making Forth readable is good use of white-space and indentation.
Your example becomes much more readable like this:
        a b +   c *   d -
You might want to check out Leo Brodies' Thinking Forth for more ideas
on making Forth readable.

>4) all the stack gymnastics involved. It seems like I spend more time 
>   trying to remember what's where on the stack than on any other aspect 
>   of forth program development.

to do too much.  Factor the words into smaller chunks.  Use the ordinary

Sometimes I use Forth to prototype my project and redo it in C later if

Being primarly a C programmer, my opinion below may be of limited
helpfulness and accuracy, but I would like to defend FORTH and
threaded languages in general.

> 1) zillions of "words" to memorize, as opposed to a small, powerful 
>    set of keywords.

What about the zillions of C libary routines you have memorized?
Like fopen, strcpy, and isascii, not to mention printf, fprintf,
vfprintf, ect.

> 2) lack (in most implementations) of local variables. (JForth does 
>    support these, actually, but I haven't played with them yet)

The idea is that you don't need local variables.  Put every thing on
the stack.  I suspect that you are writing fairly long routines (ok
C style, poor FORTH style).

> 3) lack of syntax, readability. I've been considering writing a lisp-like
>    parser for it:  ' a b + c * d - ' becomes (- (* c (+ a b)) d)

Personnally, I like this idea.  (Aren't there any threaded
interpretters of LISP out already?).  Since you like LISP, with
regard to (2), good threaded code (FORTH) is a lot like good LISP
code - no local variables, short routines.

> 4) all the stack gymnastics involved. It seems like I spend more time
>    trying to remember what's where on the stack ...

In the vein (1), think about all the C parameter sequences you have
memorized, like puts takes the FILE* last, and fprintf takes the
FILE* first.

> 5) isolationism; it's too integrated. I'd like to be able to produce
>    binary objects and libraries that can be called from other languages.

This is a problem, at least with the FORTHs I've toyed with.  My
understanding is that it was originally developed for bare hardware:
no file system, no screen driver, etc.  {BEGIN-RANT}  The continued
use of 'screens' to hold FORTH code is stupid.  Heirarchical file
systems are completely adequate for the required services.
{END-RANT}

> When will I get this religious experience I keep hearing about? Thanks.

You got to think about it differently.  Its more like lisp
programming.
